MEERA PILLAI - VOICE & WRITING STYLE GUIDE
Founder, Skinstinct
Built from 15 published pieces: 4 LinkedIn posts + 11 email newsletters (about 6,900 words)

==========================================================================
0. THE VOICE IN ONE PARAGRAPH
==========================================================================

Meera writes like a formulation scientist explaining things to a smart friend
who isn't a scientist. She is calm, exact, and a little dry. She doesn't
hype, doesn't sell, and doesn't raise her voice. She builds trust by showing
her working: specific numbers, named mechanisms, her own mistakes, and a
clear line between what the evidence shows and what it doesn't. Her
signature move is to take a label claim everyone accepts ("5% niacinamide",
"clinically tested", "natural", "SPF 30") and show that it is "the
beginning of the question, not the answer." Most pieces end by telling the
reader what question to ask, not what to buy.

Short version: Precise. Candid. Measured. Evidence first. Anti-hype.
Teaches you how to judge a claim instead of telling you what to think.


==========================================================================
1. WHO SHE IS ON THE PAGE (PERSONA)
==========================================================================

- Background: two years in pharmaceutical formulation before founding
  Skinstinct. She states her limits plainly: "I'm not a dermatologist. I
  don't have a medical degree." Her authority comes from knowing how
  ingredient systems behave, how stability documentation works, and how to
  read a CoA. She never claims expertise she doesn't have.
- Values: documentation, being able to verify claims, honesty over
  flattery, and designing for the Indian market (humidity, UV index,
  Mumbai/Chennai/Kochi/Kolkata).
- Commercial stance: she openly plays down selling. "This list is not going
  to be a place where I sell you things." She often says when she has NO
  commercial stake ("We don't currently sell a peptide product," "I'm not
  selling a sunscreen").
- She admits mistakes as data, not as drama: "it should have been right
  the first time," "it took us embarrassingly long to make the connection."
- She is fair to the other side. She won't attack people or whole
  movements. She criticises systems, gaps, and framings.

==========================================================================

  --------------------------------    --------------------------------

  Direct ("I want to start with       Blunt for effect or snarky
   something direct")

  Dry, understated humour ("It is     Jokey, punny, or cute
   not a particularly inspiring
   story.")

   for reading these emails. I mean
   that specifically, not as a
   sign-off.")
  Critical of systems                 Critical of individuals

Emotional temperature: low. Strong feelings are named flatly instead of
performed: "the specific discomfort of sitting in that room," "a frustrating
one because it's entirely solvable and mostly unaddressed."

ZERO exclamation marks across all 15 pieces. Almost no rhetorical questions
(2 question marks in the whole corpus, both real questions).

3. SIGNATURE RHETORICAL MOVES (use these; they ARE the voice)
==========================================================================

3.1  "The label says X. X alone tells you almost nothing."
     Open with a claim the reader trusts, then take it apart into the
     conditions that actually decide the result (pH, delivery base/vehicle,
     concentration, stability, batch consistency, how much you apply).
       - "That number is almost certainly meaningless without knowing
          three other things about the formulation."
       - "This is true. It is also almost entirely useless information..."

3.2  "I'm not saying A. I'm saying B." (the precision frame)
     Heads off the reader over-reading her point. Used in almost every
     piece. Variants:
       - "I am not making a case that niacinamide doesn't work... What I'm
          saying is that..."
       - "I want to be precise about what I'm not saying here."
       - "I want to be careful here because..."
       - "I'm not trying to make you distrust every product on your shelf.
          I'm trying to explain what questions to ask."

3.3  Fair to both sides, then the specific truth
     "There's a version that dismisses X entirely... and a version that
     treats X as sufficient... I think both are wrong, and the truth is
     more specific." She gives the opposing view its real merit first
     ("These are real and legitimate commercial considerations").

3.4  Separate the mechanism from the product
     "The mechanism is real... The research gap is in the translation from
     mechanism to commercial product efficacy." She sorts evidence by type:
     in-vitro vs clinical, supplier-run vs independent, 12-person
     perception study vs 500-person double-blind.

3.5  Numbered teardown: "The first thing... The second thing... The
     third thing..." or single-word topic openers: "Take penetration."
     "Then there's concentration." "Finally, stability."

3.6  Specific numbers everywhere
     pH 3.2, pH 5.5-5.8, 23% of returns, 71% from cities above 70%
     humidity, returns fell to 8%, 67% repeat purchase rate, 2mg/cm2,
     14 months, 26 allergens, 25-person patch test. She never writes
     "many" or "a lot" when she has a number.

3.7  Own-brand evidence, including failures
     "We spent several months adjusting the base..." "Our first serum base
     was too film-forming for high-humidity skin conditions." "I have been
     trying to formulate one... for about 14 months." The brand shows up
     as a case study, not an ad.

3.8  Disclose commercial interest
     "I'm telling you this because I want the assessment to be
     straightforward - I don't have a commercial stake in how you read it."

3.9  Silence or a vague answer is useful information
     Recurring closer: "If the answer is that they don't have that
     documentation, that's useful information." / "If the answer is vague
     or doesn't arrive, that is also useful information." / "...that tells
     you something useful about how closely the formulation chemistry is
     being managed."

3.10 End with the question to ask (the reader's to-do)
     Give the reader a concrete, doable check: ask for pH, CoA, stability
     data, study design, sample size, sourcing documentation, which climate
     the base was validated for. "You should ask for them. Not from us
     specifically - from any brand..."

3.11 The flat, undercutting close
     She often ends a section or piece with a short, deflating sentence:
       - "It is a documentation gap and a decision to do something about
          it."
       - "It was designed for the wrong context."
       - "The label percentage stayed the same the entire time."
       - "It is not a substitute."
       - "Strip back the routine before building it up."

3.12 Short verdict sentences after a long explanation
     "This is legal. It is also not helpful."
     "The sensitisation is permanent."
     "This is not a rounding issue."
     "The base isn't decoration around the active. It determines whether
      the active arrives."

4. SENTENCE-LEVEL MECHANICS
==========================================================================

Rhythm
- Average sentence is about 17 words (median 14). About 1 in 6 sentences is
  6 words or fewer; about 1 in 12 runs past 35 words.
- Typical pattern: one or two long, carefully qualified explanatory
  sentences, then a short blunt one that lands the point.
    "Niacin is not inert. It causes vasodilation."
    "Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal. It is
     also not helpful."

Paragraphs
- 2-6 sentences, one idea each. White space between every paragraph.
- A one-sentence paragraph is used for a turn or verdict ("The longer
  version." / "I asked about the pH of the finished formula. The answer
  was 3.2.").

Punctuation
- Spaced hyphen " - " as the dash, for asides and lists inside a sentence
  (about 55 uses). NOT em dashes. e.g. "...by customers, by other brands,
  and by us in our early formulations - that it's worth going through
  properly."
- Colon to set up a reveal or a list: "The honest answer is: it depends
  on the pH of both products..."
- Parentheses for technical clarifiers: "(participants rating their own
  skin feel)", "(Matrixyl)".
- Quotation marks around claims she is examining: "clinically tested",
  "natural", "barrier repair", "contains 7 peptides".
- No exclamation marks. No emojis. No ALL CAPS except when quoting someone
  else's banner. No hashtags.

Contractions
- Uses them naturally (I'm, don't, it's, that's, you're), but switches to
  the full form for emphasis: "It is not a particularly inspiring story."
  "I am not making a case that..."

Person
- "I" for judgement, opinion, experience (the most frequent pronoun).
- "We" for Skinstinct's formulation work and decisions.
- "You" for the reader's action: what to check, what to ask.

Spelling: BRITISH / INDIAN ENGLISH
- oxidise, sensitisation, sensitise, moisturiser, colour, behaviour,
  standardised, categorisation, organisation, centimetre, maximise,
  oestrogenic.

Numbers
- Numerals for data, percentages, pH, concentrations, durations in data
  ("8 to 10 months", "4-6 months", "14 months", "2mg").
- Ranges with a hyphen: "4-5%", "pH 2.5-3.5", "20-40%".
- Numbers written as words are fine in casual phrasing ("two years",
  "fifteen times", "five to ten minutes").

5. VOCABULARY
==========================================================================

Words and phrases she uses often (her lexicon)
  specific / specifically (27 uses)   actually (11)      meaningful (11)
  documentation (11)                  honest / honestly  genuinely
  the evidence / the research         properly           useful information
  formulation (level/chemistry/       base / vehicle /   concentration
    conditions/reality)                 delivery system
  stability / stability testing       CoA                INCI
  pH                                  batch              contract manufacturer
  "the gap between X and Y"           "worth + -ing" (worth investigating,
                                        worth going through properly)
  "I want to..." (12 uses: I want to explain / be precise / be careful /
    be honest / start with something direct / fix that)

Technical terms: use them, explain them once in plain words, move on.
  "Niacin... causes vasodilation. In skin terms, that's the flushing
   reaction..." / "transepidermal water loss increases - water evaporates
   from the skin faster than it should"

Words and phrases she NEVER uses (avoid)
  - Hype and beauty-marketing language: glow, radiant, flawless, miracle,
    game-changer, holy grail, skin-loving, pamper, luxurious, "your skin
    will thank you", obsessed, must-have, revolutionary.
  - Sales CTAs: shop now, limited time, don't miss out, link in bio,
    use code...
  - Absolutes without evidence: always, never, guaranteed, 100%.
  - Fear-mongering: toxic, chemicals (as a scary word), dangerous (unless
    backed; she actually says "The product isn't dangerous").
  - LinkedIn clichés: "Here's the thing", "Let that sink in", "Agree?",
    "Thoughts?", one-line-per-sentence broetry, emoji bullets, hashtags.
  - Exclamation marks.

6. STRUCTURE TEMPLATES
==========================================================================

6.1 LINKEDIN POST (about 450-600 words, prose paragraphs, no headers,
    no bullets, no hashtags, no sign-off)

  1. Hook = a concrete scene or fact, not a question.
       - A claim: "The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the
         ingredient listed at 5% or 10% on the label. That number is
         almost certainly meaningless without knowing three other things."
       - A dated scene: "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review
         meeting..." / "Last September I was at a trade fair in Mumbai."
       - A data point: "In the 12 months to June 2025, 23% of our product
         returns..."
  2. Why it matters / what's really going on (mechanism, in plain words).
  3. The teardown (first / second / third thing, or a list of cases).
  4. The precision caveat ("I'm not saying... I'm saying...").
  5. What Skinstinct did, including the cost or trade-off ("adds to our
     timelines and costs more than the industry standard approach").
  6. Close: the question the reader should ask, OR a flat one-line
     summary. Never "What do you think?"

6.2 EMAIL NEWSLETTER (about 450-750 words)

  Subject line: plain, lower-key, often with a candid aside or a two-part
  statement. No clickbait, no emojis.
    "What you're actually signed up for"
    "pH - the question nobody puts on the label"
    "The Vitamin C email (I've been putting this off)"
    "Peptides: what the research actually says"
    "Clean beauty gets some things right. The rest is making things harder."
    "18 months of Skinstinct. Some data, some honesty."

  Greeting: "Hi," on its own line. Always. (No name, no "Hey!", no
  "Dear reader".)

  Opening: one of
    - A plain statement of what this email is and why: "I want to talk
      about how actives work, because I think most explainer content on
      this topic stops exactly one step before the part that matters."
    - A candid note on the writing: "I've been avoiding writing this one
      because..." / "I almost didn't write this email, because..."
    - A reader question: "A customer wrote to me last month asking..."
    - The TL;DR first: "The shortest version of this email: ... The
      longer version."

  Body: define the thing → explain the mechanism → show where the label
  and reality split → give numbers → what we do / don't do and why.

  Honesty beat: "I want to be honest about something: we don't currently
  sell a Vitamin C product."

  Practical takeaway: "Here's what I want you to take from this." / "What
  this means practically:" / "What to look for, if you want to use
  peptides:" / "The practical implication:"

  Sign-off: just "Meera" on its own line. No "xoxo", no "Cheers", no
  title, no P.S. selling anything.

6.3 CONTENT CATEGORIES SHE WRITES IN
  - Ingredient Deep-Dive (niacinamide, Vitamin C, peptides)
  - Formulation Science (actives, pH, ceramides/barrier)
  - Industry Transparency ("clinically tested", "natural", clean beauty)
  - India-Specific Context (humidity, UV index, climate-validated bases)
  - Brand Philosophy (why no fragrance, what the list is for)
  - Consumer Education (SPF application)
  - Founder Story (the 2021 stability meeting, 18-month retrospective)

7. HOW SHE HANDLES EVIDENCE AND UNCERTAINTY
==========================================================================

- Calibrated claims: "solid and replicable across well-controlled
  studies" vs "the evidence base is different" vs "essentially absent"
  vs "thinner". She matches her words to how strong the evidence is.
- She says when she doesn't know: "I don't know how to separate 'the
  formulation works' from 'the communication builds trust' in that
  number, and probably both are contributing."
- She won't claim more than her data supports, even about her own brand:
  "We don't currently market any of our products primarily on barrier
  repair claims. When we have the formulation data to support that claim
  specifically, we will."
- She goes against popular opinion when the evidence says so, and says
  where her own practice differs from her reasoning: "I don't use parabens
  ... But I don't avoid them because I believe they're dangerous at
  cosmetic concentrations, because the evidence doesn't support that."
- "Legal" is not the same as "helpful": she separates what regulations
  allow from what customers need to know, without calling anyone a
  liar. "This is not a loophole - it's the current state of the
  regulatory framework."

DON'T

  [ ] No "glow", "miracle", "holy grail", "skin-loving", "game-changer"
  [ ] No hard sell or discount CTA; product launches are explained, not

      synthetic-bad framing
  [ ] No blaming individual people or competitors by name
  [ ] No claiming dermatologist/medical authority
  [ ] No rhetorical-question hooks ("Ever wondered why...?")
  [ ] No one-sentence-per-line LinkedIn formatting
  [ ] No over-inflated conclusions; keep the ending understated

9. BEFORE / AFTER EXAMPLE
==========================================================================

GENERIC SKINCARE BRAND VOICE (not Meera):
  "✨ Say hello to glowing skin! ✨ Our NEW hyaluronic acid serum is packed
  with 5 types of HA to deeply hydrate and plump your skin. Dermatologist
  approved and 100% clean! Your skin will thank you 💧 #skincare #glow"

MEERA'S VOICE:
  "Most hyaluronic acid serums list the ingredient without telling you the
  molecular weight. That number matters more than the percentage.

  High molecular weight HA sits on the skin surface and holds water there.
  Lower molecular weight forms get further into the upper layers of the
  epidermis. Neither is 'better' - they do different jobs. A label that
  says 'contains 5 types of hyaluronic acid' tells you the formulator used
  five ingredients. It doesn't tell you how much of each, or whether the
  base lets any of them do what the marketing implies.

  There's also a climate question. In a dry climate, HA pulls water from
  the air and the deeper skin layers into the surface. In Mumbai in July,
  with humidity above 80%, it behaves very differently, and the texture
  our customers describe changes with it.

  I'm not saying hyaluronic acid doesn't work. The evidence for surface
  hydration is solid. I'm saying the ingredient name is the beginning of
  the question, not the answer.

  If you want to know what you're buying, ask the brand for the molecular
  weight ranges and the concentration. If they can't tell you, that's
  useful information."

(Note: the example above shows the style only. The HA facts in it are
illustrative and should be checked before anyone publishes them.)
